GRAIN: po

The value of "grain" is "po". The value of "unit_id" is the purchase order number.

Allowed anomaly types for this grain: "duplicate_payment", "approval_bypass", "abnormal_amount", or null.

WHAT THE RECORD CONTAINS

For this purchase order you are given: its identifier, state, vendor, and the buyer who raised it; its total amount and its order and approval dates; its line items, each with the quantity ordered, the unit price paid, the item's standard cost, the quantities received and invoiced, and — where this vendor has supplied the same item before — the average unit price it charged for that item previously; the goods receipts recorded against it; the vendor bills and the payments; a timeline of events with the user who performed each one; the supplier's own invoice reference recorded on each of this order's bills; and this vendor's other purchase orders, each listed with its bills' document numbers, invoice references, amounts and dates; and neutral comparisons — the approval threshold, how many orders this vendor has received and their average value, the counts of bills and payments, and the received quantity against the billed quantity.

DUPLICATE_PAYMENT

The same purchase order should normally be matched by one vendor bill and settled by one payment. Look for signs that the same goods were billed or paid more than once.

Compare the number of bills and payments recorded for this order against what the order and the goods receipt justify. Two bills that each separately cover the full order value are duplication. A payment that leaves the vendor paid more than the order is worth is the strongest signal available for this anomaly.

A supplier is paid once for each obligation it has raised, and the supplier's own invoice reference is what identifies that obligation — one reference, one obligation, whatever it is billed against. So this check does not stop at the boundary of this order. Where a bill on this order carries an invoice reference that also appears on a bill of one of this vendor's other orders shown here, one obligation has been recorded in two places and the vendor stands to be paid for it twice. Checking a supplier's invoice reference against what has already been booked for that supplier is an ordinary accounts-payable control and needs no other evidence to support it. Read the reference itself and not the money: two orders of one vendor carrying similar amounts at similar dates are ordinary trade and are not evidence of this anomaly on their own, and two records of one obligation may differ by a few satang through rounding, so an exact amount match is neither required nor sufficient.

A second record of one obligation does not always look identical to the first. The same invoice may be entered again under a separate supplier record for the same firm, or with its reference altered just enough that a check looking for an exact match does not connect the two — a changed separator, an added character, different capitalisation. Treat a reference that is nearly the same as one already recorded as the same reference, unless the record explains the difference. The same delivery can also reach payment by two routes, billed once by the supplier and once again through whoever received the goods; the route a bill took does not make it a separate obligation.

The plainest test is the money. A vendor left paid more than the order obliges, or paid again against a bill already settled, has been paid twice however the second record was made to look.

APPROVAL_BYPASS

Recall the approval rule: at or below the threshold, the buyer confirms the order alone, with no separate approval step. Because of this, the approver and the buyer being the same person is normal and expected below the threshold. It is not an anomaly by itself and must not be reported as one.

This condition becomes meaningful only above the threshold. Above the threshold, the buyer approving their own order is the anchor signal. Supporting context that strengthens this signal includes: a very short interval between the order being placed and the order being approved; a vendor that was created shortly before the order was placed; or a high order value.

Above the threshold, an order that never went out for quotation before it was approved can strengthen a self-approval finding, but does not establish one on its own. At or below the threshold, the same omission is a legitimate process variant and is not an anomaly.

An approval is only a control if the approver actually reviewed the order before approving it. Where an approval was granted in a time too short for any review of a commitment of that size to have taken place, the control was recorded as performed but was not substantively performed.

Who approved, and when, matter alongside whether an approval exists. An approval given by someone who does not ordinarily approve this vendor's orders, or orders of this size, departs from the usual course, and the record should account for it. An approval recorded after the goods arrived, after the bill was posted, or after the payment was made cannot have been the check it appears to be — it records a decision already carried out.

ABNORMAL_AMOUNT

The anchor signal is the price of an individual line, not the size of the order.

For each line, compare the unit price paid against two references: the item's standard cost, and, where it is given, the average unit price this same vendor charged for this same item before. The vendor's own price history is the more demanding of the two. A supplier that has been charging one price for an item and now charges markedly more has to be explained by something the record actually shows.

Prices move for ordinary reasons, and a line sitting a few per cent above a reference is showing that ordinary movement. A difference that small is not weak evidence of this anomaly; it is evidence of ordinary pricing, and it does not support a finding on its own, nor together with other lines doing the same. The finding requires a price standing clearly apart from its references, by a margin routine price movement does not account for.

An order can be small in total and still carry an over-priced line. Do not let an order total that looks ordinary against the vendor's average outweigh a line that does not. A large order total is likewise not a finding in itself: the order-level comparison is weak evidence and should never carry a finding alone.

Where an order has several lines, compare them with one another. A line that departs from its references while its neighbours sit close to theirs is a strong signal. A single-line order can carry this anomaly just as well, and must be judged on its own references.

Evidence against a finding: a quantity, product specification, or delivery arrangement recorded on the order that would account for the price; a first purchase of an item from this vendor, where no earlier price exists to compare against; a price sitting close to both references.

Do not explain away a price by supposing an urgent need, a rush order, or a special arrangement that the record does not show. Where nothing in the record accounts for the difference, the unexplained difference is the finding.

An inflated price is also how an arrangement between a buyer and a supplier gets funded: the excess has to be raised somewhere, and the purchase is where it is raised. That is why an unexplained price carries weight even when everything else about the order looks ordinary.
